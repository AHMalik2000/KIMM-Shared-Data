Recap from Lecture I

What We Know So Far

In the previous lecture we established that a Gaussian Process (GP) is a distribution over functions, fully specified by a mean function $\mu(x)$ and a kernel (covariance) function $k(x, x')$:

$$f(x) \sim \mathcal{GP}\!\bigl(\mu(x),\; k(x, x')\bigr)$$

Given observed data, the GP posterior gives us at every point:

  • A mean prediction $\mu(x)$ — our best estimate of $f(x)$
  • A predictive variance $\sigma^2(x)$ — our calibrated uncertainty

Today's lecture takes these GP capabilities as given and asks: how do we use them to optimise an expensive function?

What is Bayesian Optimisation
and why do we need it?

3

The Optimisation Problem

We seek the global optimum of an objective function:

$$x^{*} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f(x)$$

where $f$ has properties that make classical methods impractical:

  • Expensive to evaluate — each call may be a CFD simulation (hours), a physical experiment (days), or a neural-network training run (GPU-hours)
  • Black-box — no closed-form expression is available; we only observe input-output pairs $(x_i, y_i)$
  • No gradient information — derivatives of $f$ are unavailable, so gradient-based optimisers cannot be used
  • Possibly noisy — observations may be corrupted: $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$
4

Why Not Brute Force?

When evaluations are cheap, we can afford exhaustive strategies. When they are expensive, we cannot:

MethodStrategyEvaluations neededUses past data?
Grid searchEvaluate on a fixed grid$O(k^d)$ — exponential in dimension $d$No
Random searchSample uniformly at randomMany, high varianceNo
Gradient descentFollow $\nabla f$ModerateRequires gradients
Bayesian OptimisationModel $f$, then decideVery few (typically 10–100)Yes

BO's advantage: it builds a statistical model of $f$ and uses all previous evaluations to decide where to sample next.

5

The Two Ingredients of BO

Bayesian Optimisation combines two components in a sequential loop:

1. Surrogate Model

A cheap probabilistic approximation of $f(x)$ that provides predictions with uncertainty.

Answers: "What do we think $f$ looks like?"

2. Acquisition Function

A utility function $\alpha(x)$ that quantifies the value of evaluating $f$ at a candidate point $x$.

Answers: "Where should we sample next?"

Together they form a sequential decision process: model → decide → evaluate → update → repeat.

The Surrogate Model

7

What is a Surrogate Model?

Definition

A surrogate model (also: metamodel, emulator, response surface) is a statistical model that approximates an expensive function $f(x)$ from a small set of observations $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$.

For BO, the surrogate must provide two outputs at every query point $x$:

  • Predictive mean $\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(x) \mid \mathcal{D}_n]$ — the best estimate
  • Predictive uncertainty $\sigma(x) = \sqrt{\text{Var}[f(x) \mid \mathcal{D}_n]}$ — how confident the estimate is

The uncertainty is critical: it is what allows BO to distinguish "explored" regions from "unexplored" ones and to make principled decisions about where to sample next.

8

Why GPs Are the Default Surrogate

Many models can predict $\mu(x)$, but few give well-calibrated $\sigma(x)$. GPs are the standard choice because:

  • Closed-form posterior — predictions are exact (no sampling needed), computed via the conditioning formulae from Lecture I
  • Calibrated uncertainty — $\sigma(x)$ is analytically derived, not heuristic; it shrinks exactly at observed points and grows in unobserved regions
  • Non-parametric — the model complexity grows with the data, not fixed in advance
  • Data-efficient — perform well with very few observations ($n < 50$), which is exactly the regime where BO operates

Other surrogates (Random Forests, Bayesian Neural Networks, etc.) can be used, but GPs remain the most common choice for low-dimensional BO.

Acquisition Functions

11

What is an Acquisition Function?

Definition

An acquisition function $\alpha(x)$ is a cheap-to-evaluate utility that scores every candidate point $x$ based on how valuable it would be to evaluate $f(x)$ there. The next sample is chosen as $x_{n+1} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(x)$.

The acquisition function uses the GP posterior ($\mu$ and $\sigma$) as its input — it never calls the expensive $f$ itself. Different acquisition functions encode different strategies for balancing two competing goals:

  • Exploitation — sample where $\mu(x)$ is already high (refine what we know)
  • Exploration — sample where $\sigma(x)$ is large (learn where we are ignorant)

In this lecture we will cover the three most widely used acquisition functions:

FunctionAbbreviationCore idea
Expected ImprovementEIHow much improvement can we expect?
Probability of ImprovementPIHow likely is any improvement?
Upper Confidence BoundUCBWhat is the optimistic upper bound on $f$?
12

The Exploration–Exploitation Trade-off

Before diving into the formulae, let us build intuition for the central tension that every acquisition function must resolve:

Exploitation

Sample where the predicted mean $\mu(x)$ is high — regions we already believe are good.

Risk: converging to a local optimum and missing the global one, because we never looked elsewhere.

Exploration

Sample where the uncertainty $\sigma(x)$ is large — regions we haven't explored.

Risk: wasting expensive evaluations in regions that turn out to be uninteresting.

A good acquisition function balances both signals. Too much exploitation = stuck in a local basin. Too much exploration = slow convergence. The three functions we cover next differ mainly in how they strike this balance.

13

Shared Notation

Before we define each acquisition function, let us establish the shared notation. All three build on the same GP posterior quantities:

SymbolMeaning
$\mu(x)$GP posterior mean at $x$
$\sigma(x)$GP posterior standard deviation at $x$
$f^* = \max_{i=1,\ldots,n} y_i$Best observed value so far (the "incumbent")
$\Phi(\cdot)$Standard normal CDF — $\Phi(z) = P(Z \leq z)$
$\phi(\cdot)$Standard normal PDF — $\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$
$Z$Standardised improvement: $Z = \dfrac{\mu(x) - f^*}{\sigma(x)}$

With these in hand, each acquisition function is just a different way of combining $\mu$, $\sigma$, and $f^*$.

14

Expected Improvement (EI)

Definition

EI measures the expected amount by which a new evaluation will improve over the current best $f^*$.

$$\text{EI}(x) = \begin{cases} (\mu(x) - f^*)\,\Phi(Z) + \sigma(x)\,\phi(Z) & \text{if } \sigma(x) > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } \sigma(x) = 0 \end{cases}$$

$$\text{where}\quad Z = \frac{\mu(x) - f^*}{\sigma(x)}$$

Interpreting the two terms:

  • $(\mu(x) - f^*)\,\Phi(Z)$ — exploitation: large when the mean is well above $f^*$ and improvement is likely
  • $\sigma(x)\,\phi(Z)$ — exploration: large when uncertainty is high, even if the mean is not promising
15

Where Does the EI Formula Come From?

Define the improvement random variable at point $x$ as:

$$I(x) = \max\bigl(f(x) - f^*,\; 0\bigr)$$

EI is the expectation of $I(x)$ under the GP posterior, where $f(x) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu(x), \sigma^2(x)\bigr)$:

$$\text{EI}(x) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[I(x)\bigr] = \int_{f^*}^{\infty} (f - f^*)\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(f \mid \mu(x), \sigma^2(x)\bigr)\;df$$

Evaluating this integral analytically (by substituting $z = \frac{f - \mu}{\sigma}$ and integrating by parts) yields the closed-form expression on the previous slide.

This closed form is one reason EI is so popular: it is cheap to compute and differentiable with respect to $x$, making it easy to optimise.

16

Visualising the Two Terms of EI

The figure below decomposes EI into its constituent parts on a GP fitted to the initial 3 observations, making it possible to see how the exploitation and exploration terms contribute at each point $x$:

  • Left (red): the exploitation term $(\mu - f^*)\Phi(Z)$ — peaks where the mean already exceeds $f^*$
  • Middle (blue): the exploration term $\sigma\,\phi(Z)$ — peaks in high-uncertainty regions
  • Right (green): the full EI — the sum of both, selecting a point that balances the two signals
17

Probability of Improvement (PI)

Having seen how EI balances explore and exploit, let us now look at a simpler alternative:

Definition

PI measures the probability that a new evaluation will exceed the current best by at least a margin $\xi \geq 0$.

$$\text{PI}(x) = P\bigl(f(x) > f^* + \xi\bigr) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu(x) - f^* - \xi}{\sigma(x)}\right)$$

Key properties:

  • PI asks only whether improvement occurs, not how much — a tiny improvement and a huge one are treated identically
  • With $\xi = 0$: very greedy — samples cluster near the current best, risking premature convergence
  • Increasing $\xi$ adds a margin that encourages more exploration, but the right value is problem-dependent
  • PI was one of the earliest acquisition functions proposed (Kushner, 1964)
18

Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)

UCB takes an entirely different approach from EI and PI. Rather than reasoning about improvement over $f^*$, it constructs an optimistic estimate of the function itself:

Definition

UCB scores each point by its mean plus an exploration bonus proportional to the uncertainty.

$$\text{UCB}(x) = \mu(x) + \kappa\,\sigma(x)$$

Key properties:

  • $\kappa > 0$ directly controls the balance: $\kappa = 0$ is pure exploitation; $\kappa \to \infty$ is pure exploration
  • Also called GP-UCB; has theoretical regret bounds: $O(\sqrt{T\,\gamma_T})$ where $\gamma_T$ is the information gain (Srinivas et al., 2010)
  • Common practical choice: $\kappa = 2$ (roughly a 95% upper confidence bound)
19

Practical Guidance: Choosing an Acquisition Function

There is no universally best acquisition function — the right choice depends on the problem characteristics and your priorities:

If you need…UseWhy
A safe, general-purpose defaultEIParameter-free, balances both signals, works well across problems
Fast convergence on a simple landscapePI (small $\xi$)Aggressively exploits; converges quickly if the basin is easy to find
Robustness to multiple local optimaUCB (high $\kappa$)Strong exploration prevents premature convergence
Theoretical guaranteesUCBFormal regret bounds under mild assumptions

In practice, EI is the most widely used default. UCB is preferred in research settings where theoretical properties matter. We will now see how all of these fit into the complete BO algorithm.

How It All Comes Together

23

From Components to Algorithm

We have now covered the two building blocks — the surrogate model and the acquisition function. Bayesian Optimisation puts them together in a simple iterative loop:

  • Step 1. Start with a small initial dataset (e.g. 3–5 random evaluations)
  • Step 2. Fit the surrogate to all data collected so far → obtain $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma(x)$
  • Step 3. Maximise the acquisition function to choose the next evaluation point $x_{n+1}$
  • Step 4. Evaluate the expensive function at $x_{n+1}$ and add the result to the dataset
  • Step 5. Go back to Step 2 until the evaluation budget is exhausted

Each iteration, the surrogate becomes more accurate and the acquisition function makes better-informed decisions. The next slide formalises this as pseudocode.

24

The Bayesian Optimisation Algorithm

Input: search space $\mathcal{X}$, budget $T$, acquisition function $\alpha$

1. Choose initial design $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{n_0}\}$ (e.g. Latin Hypercube) and evaluate $y_i = f(x_i)$

2. for $t = n_0 + 1$ to $T$ do

  a. Fit GP to data $\mathcal{D}_{t-1} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{t-1}$

  b. Compute $\alpha(x)$ using GP posterior $\mu(x), \sigma(x)$

  c. $x_t = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(x)$

  d. $y_t = f(x_t) + \varepsilon$   ← the expensive evaluation

  e. $\mathcal{D}_t = \mathcal{D}_{t-1} \cup \{(x_t, y_t)\}$

3. Return $x^* = \arg\max_{x_i \in \mathcal{D}_T} y_i$

Step 3: Computing Expected Improvement

def expected_improvement(X, gp, f_best):
  mu, sigma = gp.predict(X, return_std=True)
  sigma = np.maximum(sigma, 1e-9)  # avoid division by zero
  Z = (mu - f_best) / sigma
  ei = (mu - f_best) * norm.cdf(Z) + sigma * norm.pdf(Z)
  return ei

This is a direct translation of the closed-form EI formula into code. Note how each line maps to the maths:

  • mu - f_best computes $\mu(x) - f^*$ — the predicted improvement in the mean
  • Z = (mu - f_best) / sigma computes the standardised score $Z$ — the key to balancing explore and exploit
  • norm.cdf(Z) and norm.pdf(Z) are $\Phi(Z)$ and $\phi(Z)$ from scipy.stats
32

Step 4: The Complete BO Loop

for i in range(n_iterations):
  gp = fit_gp(X_obs, y_obs)              # step 2a: fit surrogate
  f_best = np.max(y_obs)                # current incumbent
  ei = expected_improvement(X, gp, f_best) # step 2b: compute alpha
  x_new = X[np.argmax(ei)]             # step 2c: argmax alpha
  y_new = objective(x_new)              # step 2d: EXPENSIVE call
  X_obs = np.vstack([X_obs, x_new])     # step 2e: update data
  y_obs = np.vstack([y_obs, y_new])

Each iteration maps directly to steps 2a–2e of the pseudocode. The inline comments show the correspondence. The entire BO engine is under 10 lines of Python.

Result after 7 iterations (10 total evaluations): $f(x^*) = 0.5003$ — matching the true optimum to 4 decimal places.

When to Use Bayesian Optimisation

Good fit

  • Expensive evaluations (simulations, physical experiments)
  • Low-dimensional input space (typically $d < 20$)
  • Smooth, continuous objective
  • Black-box: no gradients available
  • Budget of 10–100 evaluations

Poor fit

  • Cheap evaluations (can afford $10^3$+ calls)
  • Very high-dimensional ($d > 20$): GP cost is $O(n^3)$
  • Highly discrete or combinatorial problems
  • Gradient information available (use gradient descent)
  • Extremely noisy with no structure
38

Key Takeaways

  • Bayesian Optimisation is a sequential, model-based strategy for optimising expensive black-box functions using as few evaluations as possible
  • The surrogate model (typically a GP) provides predictions with calibrated uncertainty, enabling intelligent sampling
  • Acquisition functions formalise the exploration–exploitation trade-off:
    • EI: expected magnitude of improvement (balanced, parameter-free)
    • PI: probability of improvement (greedy, tuneable via $\xi$)
    • UCB: optimistic bound on $f$ (principled, tuneable via $\kappa$)
  • The BO loop — fit, decide, evaluate, update — converges to the optimum in very few evaluations compared to uninformed methods
